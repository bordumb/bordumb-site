By senior year of high school, I had finished enough credits that large parts
of my schedule were empty. I decided to use the time to learn another language.
The target was fluency from the beginning—not a list of travel phrases or
enough grammar to complete a semester.

The Romance languages I considered shared enough vocabulary and structure with
English that the early stages looked familiar. Familiarity was precisely what I
did not want. I wanted a language that would force me to reconstruct basic
assumptions about how writing and sentences work, so I chose Japanese.

I began with an inaccurate model of what I had chosen. I thought Japanese had
one alphabet: hiragana.

Japanese uses three writing systems together. Hiragana represents native
sounds and grammatical endings. Katakana represents the same set of sounds in
a parallel script, commonly for loanwords, names, and emphasis. Kanji supplies
characters that carry lexical meaning and often more than one possible
reading. A single sentence can require all three:

> コンビニで水を買いました。

`コンビニ` (*convenience store*) is written in katakana. `で`, `を`, and the
ending of `買いました` are hiragana. `水` (*water*) and `買` (*buy*) are kanji.
Learning only hiragana would let me pronounce part of the sentence while
leaving much of its meaning inaccessible.

## I paid the kanji cost first

I could postpone kanji and remain dependent on phonetic transcriptions, or I
could absorb the largest initial cost before it became an excuse. I chose the
second route and spent roughly a year cramming characters.

My main structure came from James Heisig's
[*Remembering the Kanji*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remembering_the_Kanji).
The first volume does not teach characters in newspaper frequency or school
grade order. It introduces reusable visual components, assigns them keywords,
and orders the characters so that earlier components can support later ones.
It also separates writing and keyword recall from Japanese readings. That made
the first year lopsided: I could recognize the structure of many characters
before I knew how to say them.

The routine repeated the same operations:

1. learn a component and a concrete keyword;
2. combine known components into a new character;
3. attach the combination to a short visual story;
4. recall the written character from the keyword;
5. review failures until the path from keyword to form held.

The smallest combinations made the method easy to see:

| Form | Working image | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `木` | one tree | tree |
| `林` | trees beside one another | woods |
| `森` | trees accumulating above and below | forest |

The point was not that every character behaves like a literal picture
equation. Many do not. The visible pieces gave me addresses at which memory
could store them.

Some components also create families that remain recognizable even when the
character becomes unfamiliar:

| Component | Characters | Connection |
|---|---|---|
| `金` | `銀` silver, `銅` copper, `鉄` iron | metals and metallic materials |
| `魚` | `鮭` salmon, `鯛` sea bream, `鯨` whale | fish and sea animals |
| `木` | `板` board, `枝` branch, `植` plant | wood, trees, and things made or done with them |

These components are clues rather than definitions. `鯨`, for example,
contains the fish component even though a whale is a mammal. The character
records a writing tradition, not a biological taxonomy. A clue can still
reduce an unfamiliar square of strokes into parts I already know.

  Heisig gave me a visual retrieval system, not spoken Japanese. I still had to
  learn readings, vocabulary, and usage, but new words could attach to
  characters I no longer treated as undifferentiated marks.
</Callout>

Front-loading kanji changed every later resource. A dictionary entry could
connect a sound to a form I recognized. A caption could expose the word inside
fast speech. A grammar example could show where a stem ended and an inflection
began. The year of isolated character study began returning time only after I
moved into sentences.

## Grammar turned characters into sentences

After kanji, I worked through Seiichi Makino and Michio Tsutsui's
[*A Dictionary of Japanese Grammar* series](https://store.jtpublishing.co.jp/en/pages/n-bunpoujiten):

- [Basic](https://store.jtpublishing.co.jp/en/products/1909), for core sentence
  structures, particles, conjugation, topic, and omission;
- [Intermediate](https://store.jtpublishing.co.jp/en/products/775), for
  compound patterns, discourse, and distinctions between similar expressions;
- [Advanced](https://store.jtpublishing.co.jp/en/products/1295), for the
  constructions and register differences that become visible in longer speech
  and writing.

The books are dictionaries rather than a single course. Each entry defines a
construction, shows what can attach to it, gives example sentences, and
separates it from nearby constructions. That format suited self-study because
I could follow one question into another instead of waiting for a curriculum
to introduce it.

Japanese verbs also gave me a small number of transformation systems that
could be reused across a large vocabulary. Once I knew that `食べる` belongs to
the *ichidan* pattern, its stem stayed fixed while the ending changed:

| Function | Form |
|---|---|
| non-past | `食べる` |
| negative | `食べない` |
| past | `食べた` |
| connective / request base | `食べて` |

The *godan* pattern moves the final kana through a fixed set of rows:

| Function | `書く` (*write*) |
|---|---|
| non-past | `書く` |
| negative | `書かない` |
| past | `書いた` |
| connective / request base | `書いて` |

`する` (*do*) and `来る` (*come*) carry the two major irregular paradigms:

| Verb | Negative | Past | て-form |
|---|---|---|---|
| `する` | `しない` | `した` | `して` |
| `来る` | `来ない` | `来た` | `来て` |

There are smaller exceptions—`行く` has the て-form `行って`, and a few
honorific verbs contract in their own ways—but they sit around regular systems
rather than replacing them. Because `する` and `来る` occur constantly, their
irregular forms also receive enough repetition to become automatic.

Particles made sentence roles visible without requiring English word order.
In this sentence, `は` marks the topic, `と` marks a companion, and `を` marks
the object:

> 私は昨日、友達と寿司を食べた。  
> *As for me, yesterday, with a friend, sushi, ate.*

The time, topic, and companion can move when emphasis or context changes.
Their particles continue to identify their roles, while the verb normally
anchors the end of the clause. The order is flexible, not arbitrary.

English changes its machinery when a statement becomes a question:

> You eat fish.  
> Do you eat fish?

The auxiliary `do` appears before the subject. Japanese can preserve the core
order and add a question marker:

> 魚を食べます。  
> 魚を食べますか。

The second sentence appends `か`; it does not invert the subject and verb. In
casual speech, intonation and context can carry the same job without `か`.
Once the particle and conjugation systems were familiar, assembling a
grammatical sentence required fewer decisions about position than English had
led me to expect.

## I listened before I could follow

Knowing how a sentence works did not make native speech arrive at textbook
speed. I started listening to podcasts while understanding less than half of
what was said. A minute of speech might yield one noun, a verb ending, and a
phrase I had heard before. Those fragments were enough to keep a foothold in
the audio.

Waiting for complete comprehension would have delayed the listening practice
needed to produce comprehension. Repeated exposure taught me where one phrase
ended, which sounds disappeared in casual speech, and which expressions people
used to buy time or change direction. The unknown parts did not need to become
known in a single pass.

For television, I chose programs I would have watched in English. That usually
meant comedy. Interest supplied repetition: recurring performers, familiar
formats, physical reactions, and running jokes made the next line easier to
predict than disconnected study dialogue.

Japanese comedy programs also use abundant on-screen text. Captions repeat a
punchline, label a reaction, or emphasize the word that controls the joke.
Because I had already studied kanji, the screen could resolve audio that was
too fast for my ear. I would see a known character, connect it to the sound
that had just passed, and recognize the same word more quickly the next time.

This was less an act of pretending than a choice about the threshold for
participation. I did not require every sentence to be fully understood before
letting Japanese occupy the same parts of the day that English already did.

## Each skill increased the return on the others

The parts did not improve independently. Kanji made captions and written
examples legible. Grammar divided those examples into reusable structures.
Pronunciation and repeated listening made the structures detectable in speech.
Native material then supplied vocabulary and repetitions for all three.

That loop explains why language learning can feel flat for months and then
accelerate. Ten new characters do little by themselves. Ten characters that
unlock a caption, reinforce a spoken word, and recur inside a grammar example
generate several kinds of practice from the same encounter.

It also changed how I approach other self-taught subjects. I look for the
bottleneck that makes later material inaccessible and pay that cost early. I
begin using the real medium before my understanding is complete. I choose
material I would return to without an obligation to study. Most importantly, I
treat partial recognition as a mechanism for accumulating context, not as
evidence that I should wait.

I began Japanese seventeen years ago because I had spare time and wanted a
language far enough from English to remain unfamiliar. Fluency was the stated
goal. The method that survived was more specific: learn the writing system
that makes input visible, learn the rules that connect it, then spend enough
time with incomplete understanding for recognition to compound.
